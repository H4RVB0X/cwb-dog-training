Ever looked at your dog sprawled upside down on the sofa, snoring like a rusty tractor, and wondered, “How on earth would you have survived in the wild?”

It is a fair question! After all, our dogs’ ancestors were wild predators, hunting for food and surviving in a world where life was far from easy. Fast-forward thousands of years, and many of their descendants are more concerned about missing dinner, finding the comfiest bed, or getting their human to throw a ball for the 47th time.

So, how did we get from wild wolves to the lovable, occasionally bonkers companions we share our homes with today? The answer lies in a fascinating process called domestication.

Domestication is “…the process of hereditary reorganization of wild animals and plants into domestic and cultivated forms according to the interests of people.” (www.britannica.com, accessed 20/10/2016)

But the story of how it all began is even more interesting than the definition suggests!

## It all started with wolves… but how?

Scientists agree that modern dogs descended from an ancient, now-extinct wolf population. Today’s grey wolves are their closest living relatives, although they are not the direct ancestors of our dogs.

Exactly how the relationship between humans and dogs began is still debated by academics. However, there are two popular theories about how these unlikely best friends first came together.

### Theory 1: Did humans adopt a wolf puppy?

Imagine an ancient human coming across a cute, fluffy wolf pup and thinking, “Aww, look at that face! I’m keeping you!”

It’s a lovely thought, but perhaps not quite how it happened.

One theory suggests that humans captured young wolf pups, raised them around people, and gradually bred the calmer individuals together. Over many generations, this could have helped create animals better suited to living alongside humans.

The problem? Wolves are not exactly oversized Labradors! They can be extremely challenging to tame, and raising a wild wolf by hand doesn’t automatically produce a domesticated animal. Even a wolf raised by humans can retain many of its wild instincts.

So, while humans may have raised wolf pups at various points in history, this probably isn’t the whole story.

### Theory 2: Wolves discovered the takeaway!

This is my preferred belief…

Picture an ancient human settlement. Hunters are preparing food, scraps are being discarded and somewhere nearby, a hungry wolf is thinking, “Why chase dinner when dinner is already in the rubbish pile?”

As humans began settling in one place, their food waste may have attracted wolves that were brave enough to approach. The less fearful animals could scavenge near people, finding an easier source of food than hunting alone.

Those wolves were more likely to survive and reproduce around human settlements. Over many generations, natural selection may have favoured animals that were calmer and more tolerant of humans.

Eventually, these populations became increasingly distinct from their wild relatives.

In other words, some wolves may have discovered that hanging around humans was a rather good deal!

This is often called self-domestication. It doesn’t mean wolves consciously decided to become pets, of course. Rather, the wolves best suited to living near people gained a survival advantage.

And just like that, a relationship began that would change both species forever.

## From independent hunter to human-obsessed companion

Thousands of years of evolution have helped shape dogs into animals remarkably well suited to living with us.

Your dog’s ability to pay attention to human gestures, follow our gaze and work alongside people is one of the things that makes the relationship so special.

Of course, dogs are individuals. Some are natural people-pleasers, while others would happily ignore you in favour of an interesting smell on the pavement!

Wolves are also capable of forming strong social bonds and learning from humans, particularly when raised with intensive socialisation from an early age. However, domesticated dogs generally have an advantage when it comes to adapting to human social environments.

Their development has been shaped by generations of living alongside us.

And it’s not just their behaviour that has changed.

## Dogs versus wolves: what’s the difference?

Although dogs and wolves share a common ancestry, there are some crucial differences between them.

### 1. Growing up

Dogs and wolves develop at different rates. Wolves generally mature more quickly in some physical respects, while dogs often retain more juvenile characteristics into adulthood. These differences can influence how they learn, interact, and respond to the world around them.

### 2. Love is in the litter

Female wolves typically have one breeding season each year, usually in late winter. Domestic dogs, on the other hand, can come into season more than once a year, although the frequency varies by individual and breed.

Dogs also vary considerably in litter size, with some breeds tending to have larger litters than others.

### 3. Built for different jobs

Wolves tend to have longer legs, larger feet, narrower chests, and skulls adapted to their way of life. Their teeth and powerful jaws help them capture prey and process food.

Domestic dogs come in a remarkable range of shapes and sizes, from tiny Chihuahuas to towering Great Danes. Their bodies have been altered over time by both natural evolution and human-directed breeding.

### 4. Survival of the fittest

Life in the wild is tough. Wolves must find food, avoid danger, compete for resources, and raise their young in challenging conditions. Young wolves can die from starvation, disease, predation, and other environmental pressures.

Our pet dogs generally have a rather unique experience. They have access to regular meals, shelter, veterinary care and, if they’re lucky, a human who insists on buying them yet another bed.

That said, domestication doesn’t mean dogs no longer have instincts, emotional needs, or social requirements. They still need opportunities to explore, play, learn, sniff, and behave like dogs.

And that’s something worth remembering!

## Humans start playing matchmaker

Once dogs became established alongside humans, we began selecting animals for particular characteristics.

Did we need a dog that could help herd livestock? We favoured dogs with useful herding tendencies.

A dog that could follow a scent across challenging terrain? We selected for scenting ability and persistence.

A dog that could retrieve game, guard property, or work alongside people? We bred for the physical and behavioural traits that made those tasks possible.

Over generations, this selective breeding helped produce dogs suited to an incredible range of jobs, including hunting, herding, guarding, tracking, scent detection, assistance work, and companionship.

Today, dogs also work in roles such as police detection, search and rescue, medical assistance, and support for people with disabilities.

It’s an extraordinary partnership, built on the abilities of the dog and the needs of the human.

But there’s a key point here: selective breeding can influence behaviour, but it doesn’t make every dog a perfect example of its breed. Genetics, early experiences, learning, environment, and individual personality all play a part.

A Border Collie might have a natural talent for herding, but that doesn’t mean every Border Collie will want to spend its day rounding up sheep. Some would much rather round up tennis balls!

## Gundog, Hound, Pastoral, Terrier, Toy, Utility and Working: a world of dogs

Humans have created an astonishing variety of domestic dogs through selective breeding.

From tiny companions that can fit comfortably on your lap to giant breeds that assume most of the sofa is theirs, dogs display an incredible range of appearances and abilities.

There are hundreds of recognised dog breeds worldwide, although the exact number depends on which kennel club or organisation you ask. The Fédération Cynologique Internationale, for example, recognises a different number of breeds from the UK Kennel Club or the American Kennel Club.

Wolves, by comparison, show far less variation in body shape and size than domestic dogs, although wild wolf populations also differ according to their environment and geography.

The variety we see in dogs today is a powerful reminder of just how much humans have influenced their development.

And it all began with a remarkable relationship between two species.

## Selective breeding: would they survive in the wild?

Modern dog breeding has taken selective breeding to another level, with more varieties of ‘labracadrabracockadoodlepoopoos’ than ever before. (There is a race to produce the most obscure mix.)

Breeders select for particular physical features and behaviours that might never become established through natural selection in the wild.

We can all probably recall a time that we have heard what sounded like a steam train coming around the corner, only to be faced with a small Pug or English Bulldog puffing along. With their shortened muzzles and broad heads, these features can make natural mating and giving birth difficult, while breathing problems can affect everyday life.

Without human support, veterinary care and in some cases medical intervention, dogs with such extreme physical traits could struggle to survive and reproduce successfully in the wild.

It is a reminder that while we have created an incredible variety of dogs, our choices as breeders also come with a responsibility to put their health and welfare first.
